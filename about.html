About this concept

Thorn & Grain is not a real shop. It is a working demonstration of one, built by Phuture Digital.

What this is

Thorn & Grain is an invented South African furniture brand and a working online store built around it. The catalogue, the filters, the basket and the product pages all function — you can search the range, filter by workshop, add pieces to a basket and watch the totals change. What you cannot do is buy anything, because there is nothing behind the checkout and nothing to send.

It exists so that a business considering a site like this can click through a finished one instead of reading a proposal about it.

Where the name comes from

The thornveld is the acacia savanna that covers a large part of the country’s interior — thorn trees, hard light, hard wood. Grain is what that timber becomes once it has been through a workshop. The two words are the whole brand: the raw material and the made thing.

The idea being tested

Most furniture retail hides its suppliers. The pieces arrive anonymous, and when a supplier is swapped for a cheaper one the customer has no way of knowing. This concept inverts that: every piece names the workshop that built it, where it is, and how long it has been going.

That is a genuinely harder business to run, and the site does not pretend otherwise. It means fewer suppliers, longer lead times, and no quiet substitution when a run sells out.

What is invented, and what is not

  • The brand is invented. There is no company called Thorn & Grain.
  • The seven workshops are invented. The towns they sit in are real; the businesses are not. No real workshop has been described or represented.
  • The prices are plausible, not quoted. They are in the range South African makers charge for comparable work, but no one has priced these pieces.
  • The photography is stock. Licensed from Pexels, credited in the repository. It shows real furniture made by people who have nothing to do with this concept.
  • The star ratings are sample data. The product card in the reference design carries a rating row, so it is reproduced — but no review text is invented anywhere on this site, and no customer is quoted.

The assumptions this rests on

If this were a real business, these are the four things that would have to be true, and how each could be shown to be false:

  • That buyers will wait. Lead times of three to six weeks are quoted throughout. If shoppers abandon at the lead time, the maker-led model does not survive contact with a market trained on next-day delivery.
  • That naming the workshop is worth paying for. The premise assumes provenance carries a premium. If conversion is identical with the maker line removed, it is decoration, not a proposition.
  • That small workshops can hold quality across a run. Seven suppliers is seven quality baselines. A single bad batch damages the brand far more than it would damage an anonymous retailer.
  • That delivery maths works. Flat R750 nationally and free over R10 000 is stated on the home page and applied in the basket. On bulky furniture to a far province, that is likely to be sold below cost.
